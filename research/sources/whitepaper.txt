Abstract
We propose a mechanism for bootstrapping a generic onchain speculation game, without requiring an initial or
incentivized LP pool (also known as a bankroll). This allows for the creation of new games with no upfront capital
commitment, with a few other emergent properties such as: unconstrained bet sizing, negative house edge (+EV
bets), eventual solvency, token emissions and more...
1 Premise
1.1 History of Onchain Games of Chance
Onchain speculation games of chance have existed in the
cryptocurrency ecosystem since the beginning, offering
a blend of transparency, decentralization, and innova-
tion that traditional online gambling platforms could not
match. These games leverage blockchain technology to
provide provably fair experiences, immutable transaction
records, and often decentralized operations, appealing to
users seeking fairness and transparency.
SatoshiDice: Pioneering Bitcoin Gambling
Launched in April 2012 by Erik Voorhees, SatoshiDice
was the first major onchain speculation game. Players
sent Bitcoin to different addresses, each with a specific
probability of winning and corresponding payout. The
outcome was determined by a combination of the wa-
ger’s transaction hash and a secret salt, ensuring fair-
ness and transparency. SatoshiDice quickly became im-
mensely popular, accounting for a significant portion of
Bitcoin transactions in its early years.
Bustabit: Innovating Crash Games
Introduced in 2015, Bustabit pioneered crash gambling
games. Players bet on a multiplier that increases until
it crashes, with the option to cash out before the crash.
This introduced a social dimension to onchain gambling,
as players could see and react to each other’s bets and
decisions.
Etheroll & EtherCrash: Ethereum Iterations
With Ethereum’s smart contract capabilities, the land-
scape of onchain speculation games expanded beyond Bit-
coin. Etheroll, launched in 2017, offered a dice game
where players could bet on virtual dice rolls, using smart
contracts to allow players to set their own odds and pay-
outs, while ensuring transparency. EtherCrash, launched
in 2018, followed the crash game model, where players bet
on how long a multiplier would increase before crashing.
1.2 Limitations of Existing Mechanisms
Despite the advancements and popularity of onchain
games of chance, several limitations persist, impacting
their efficiency and user experience:
 Need for Initial Liquidity Providers (LP):
Most onchain games require an initial liquidity pool
to function. This pool ensures that players can re-
ceive payouts promptly. However, establishing this
pool can be challenging, as it requires significant ini-
tial capital.
 Incentivizing Liquidity Providers: To attract
and retain liquidity providers, platforms often need
to offer incentives such as a share of the profits or
tokens. This requirement can complicate the plat-
form’s economic model and affect its sustainability.
 Constraint on Bet Sizing: The size of bets that
players can place is often constrained by the size
of the liquidity pool. If the pool is small, it limits
the maximum possible bet, potentially discouraging
high-stakes players and limiting the game’s appeal.
 Solvency Concerns: Players must continually
evaluate the solvency of the platform they inter-
act with. Notably, EtherCrash had a bad actor
drain the LP. Platforms like Rollbit, which offer
the blockchain-based Rollercoaster game, continue to
suffer from this concern.

2 Proposal
We introduce a novel mechanism that addresses the
stated concerns, by utilizing a queue-based payout sys-
tem to allow the protocol to sustain temporary overall
insolvencies.
2.1 High Level Design Choices
The proposed mechanism allows for great flexibility with
respect to game design, allowing game creators to specify:
 payout structure and odds
 house edge (+ or -)
 a solvency ratio invariant
without having to deal with:
 initializing or incentivizing a LP pool
 constraining bet size based on the LP pool
 designing token emissions
2.2 Payout Queue System
The core of the new mechanism is a novel payout queue.
This system ensures that the protocol remains solvent
while managing payouts efficiently and fairly.
After each gameplay iteration, a protocol imple-
menting this mechanism evaluates its ability to pay out
based on a predefined solvency ratio.
The queue logic is essentially:
1. a new wager is placed and wins
2. if a queue already exists:
(a) add wager to the end of the queue
(b) evaluate whether the front of the queue can be
paid out, determined by the solvency ratio
(c) if yes, pay out the front of the queue
3. if no existing queue:
(a) evaluate whether the wager can be paid out,
determined by the solvency ratio
(b) if yes, pay out the wager
(c) otherwise, create a queue of length one
2.3 Solvency Ratio Invariant
The Solvency Ratio Invariant (SRI) defines whether the
protocol can pay out new wagers. The solvency ratio is
defined as:
Solvency Ratio = (ETH in the treasury) + (Eth in the queue)
Liabilities of the queue
(1)
The eth in the treasury is the protocol’s accumulated eth
from losses, edge, etc...
The eth in the queue is the total unpaid won input
wagers (minus any taxes).
The liabilities of the queue is the total unpaid win-
nings of the above input wagers.
At one extreme, an SRI of 1.0 would require the
protocol to be fully solvent in order to pay out any
wager. In practice, this means that anytime the protocol
would be insolvent, it will queue all subsequent won
wagers until enough losses/edge builds up to pay the
entire queue out.
At the other end, an SRI of 0.0 would indiscrimi-
nately pay out the front of the queue with subsequent
wagers.
One elegant way to construct an SRI is to
define it as:
(ETH in the treasury) + (ETH in the queue)

i=1 Wageri, where n is the queue length
(2)
With this definition, the protocol can let the queued un-
paid won wagers withdraw their input (forfeiting any win-
nings). This allows ”stuck” participants to recoup some
eth, while increasing the solvency of the remaining queue.
2.4 Yield-Bearing Wager Denominations
If the wager currency generates native yield, the queue
system will naturally increase its solvency ratio over time.
2.5 Token Emissions
Token emissions enable games with a positive house edge
to present as +EV for the player by immediately remu-
nerating them with a liquid token. The basic emission
framework features a linearly decreasing reward based on
game iteration, encouraging early participation and initi-
ating the flywheel effect.
A tax on wagers (similar to a house edge) can incen-
tivize token ownership through a buy-and-burn mecha-
nism, which will price in future cash flows. Additionally,
token emissions can be linked to queue length to incen-
tivize wagers when the protocol is temporarily insolvent
and a queue exists.

In order to better demonstrate this mechanism, here are
a few example simulations of different games of chance.
3.1 Fair Coin Flip
This protocol is defined as a perfectly fair coin flip, with
no house edge or taxes included.
The payout structure is thus:
 50% chance to win double the wager
 50% chance of total loss
We then simulate one million iterations with input fee 1.
As demonstrated, the state of the perfectly fair coin flip
game depends on the outcome variance - with no ability
to accumulate treasury via edge, the treasury is vulnera-
ble to bad runs at any point in its lifecycle. The queue
mechanism allows the protocol to absorb this variance.

4 Async vs Synchronous Games
A strong motivation for proposing this mechanism is to
remove the constraints from launching an asynchronous
game.
It is the author’s opinion that asynchronous games
are far superior to synchronous games. Synchronous
games, such as tournaments or peer-to-peer wagers
require much higher levels of coordination, which often
limits participation to those who can commit to being
available at the same time as other players. Without
enough player density, marginal participants may not be
able to play on demand.
However, the LP requirements of asynchronous games
often proves challenging to aspiring game designers, who
may not be able to provide the bankroll, or have no
desire to raise one. As a result, designers often default
to parimutuel, synchronous game designs where players
bring their own capital, thus alleviating the need for a
large initial liquidity provision.
By introducing this mechanism and removing the
LP requirement and bet size constraints, we hope to see
the creation of more novel, asynchronous games.
